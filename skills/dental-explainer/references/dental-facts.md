# Dental facts reference (correction layer)

Correction layer for drawing 3D educational dental figures. Values are population averages; real teeth vary.
Tags: **[V]** = checked against a source listed at the end (sections 1-14 audited Oct 2026; links re-checked then). **[U]** = unverified (a standard textbook value I could not confirm against a reachable source; treat as approximate).

## 1. Tooth numbering

### FDI / ISO 3950 (two-digit) [V]
- First digit = quadrant, second digit = position counted from the midline (1 = central incisor ... 8 = third molar).
- Permanent quadrants: **1** upper right, **2** upper left, **3** lower left, **4** lower right.
- Primary quadrants: **5** upper right, **6** upper left, **7** lower left, **8** lower right; positions 1-5 (central incisor ... 2nd molar). Primary teeth = 51-55, 61-65, 71-75, 81-85.
- Say the digits separately: tooth 36 is "three-six" (lower left first molar).
- Used by WHO and FDI and in most of the world (Europe, etc.).

### Universal / ADA [V]
- Permanent 1-32: **1 = upper right third molar**, counting along the upper arch to **16 = upper left third molar**, then **17 = lower left third molar** along the lower arch to **32 = lower right third molar**.
- Primary A-T: A = upper right 2nd molar ... J = upper left 2nd molar, K = lower left 2nd molar ... T = lower right 2nd molar.
- Used mainly in the USA.

### Palmer (Zsigmondy-Palmer) [V]
- A quadrant bracket symbol (┘ └ ┐ ┌) plus a number 1-8 counted from the midline (primary teeth A-E). Traditionally used in the UK, especially orthodontics.

### Mapping: permanent teeth (FDI ↔ Universal) [V]
"Right" and "left" are the **patient's** right and left.

## 2. Counts [V]
- Permanent: **32** teeth (16 per arch, 8 per quadrant), counting third molars. That is **28** if third molars are absent or extracted (agenesis is common).
- Per permanent quadrant: 2 incisors, 1 canine, 2 premolars, 3 molars (formula I2 C1 P2 M3).
- Primary: **20** teeth (10 per arch, 5 per quadrant): 2 incisors, 1 canine, 2 molars (I2 C1 M2). **There are no primary premolars.** Permanent premolars replace the primary molars.
- Permanent molars have no primary predecessors (they are "accessional" teeth).

Proportion rules for modeling [V]:
- The maxillary canine has the longest root in the dentition (about 17 mm). The canines are the longest teeth overall (about 27 mm).
- The mandibular central incisor is the narrowest tooth (MD about 5 mm). It is narrower than the mandibular lateral incisor and is *wider labiolingually than mesiodistally*.
- The maxillary central incisor is wider MD (8.5) than LL (7.0). The maxillary lateral is about 2 mm narrower and shorter than the central.
- Maxillary molars are wider BL (11) than MD (10). Mandibular molars are wider MD (11) than BL (10.5). This is a key way to tell upper and lower molars apart in an occlusal view.
- Molar MD width decreases from 1st to 2nd to 3rd molar.

## 5. Eruption ages

### Permanent (years) [V: ADA MouthHealthy / AAPD]
| Tooth | Maxillary | Mandibular |
|---|---|---|
| Central incisor | 7-8 | 6-7 |
| Lateral incisor | 8-9 | 7-8 |
| Canine | 11-12 | 9-10 (AAPD 9-11) |
| 1st premolar | 10-11 | 10-12 |
| 2nd premolar | 10-12 | 11-12 (AAPD 11-13) |
| 1st molar | 6-7 (AAPD 5.5-7) | 6-7 |
| 2nd molar | 12-13 (AAPD 12-14) | 11-13 |
| 3rd molar | 17-21 (AAPD 17-30) | 17-21 |

- The mandibular 1st molar and mandibular central incisor are usually the first permanent teeth to erupt, at about age 6.
- Mandibular teeth generally erupt before their maxillary counterparts. Premolars are a partial exception: the maxillary 1st premolar often erupts with or before the mandibular one.
- Root completion (AAPD): incisors about 9-11 y, canines 12-15 y, 1st molars 9-10 y, 2nd molars 14-16 y. The root completes about 2-3 years after eruption.

The eruption sequence for primary teeth is central incisor, lateral incisor, **1st molar, then canine**, then 2nd molar. The canine erupts after the 1st molar.

## 6. Tissue facts
- **Enamel**: thickest over cusps, about **2-2.5 mm** [V]. Some sources give up to about 3 mm. It thins to a knife or feather edge at the CEJ.
- **Composition by weight** [V]: enamel about **96% mineral** (hydroxyapatite), about 4% organic + water (non-collagenous proteins). Dentin about **70% mineral**, about 20% organic (mostly type I collagen), about 10% water. Cementum about **45-50% mineral**, about 50% organic + water. Source tables disagree slightly on the water and organic split.
- **Enamel is acellular and avascular.** Ameloblasts are lost at eruption, so enamel **cannot regenerate or repair itself biologically**. Only surface remineralization of the mineral is possible. Dentin is produced by odontoblasts that persist in the pulp, so secondary and tertiary dentin can form.
- **PDL width**: **0.15-0.38 mm** [V, Shimono et al. 2003 review]. Commonly cited average is about 0.2 mm [U]. It is **thinnest in the middle third of the root** (the fulcrum) [V] and decreases with age.
- **Biologic width / supracrestal tissue attachment (STA)** [V]: Gargiulo, Wentz & Orban 1961 (J Periodontol 32:261; 287 teeth, 30 autopsy jaws). Mean sulcus depth was **0.69 mm**, junctional epithelium **0.97 mm**, connective tissue attachment **1.07 mm**, giving **JE + CT = 2.04 mm**. The 2017 AAP/EFP World Workshop replaced the term with "supracrestal tissue attachment" (JE + supracrestal connective tissue; Jepsen et al. 2018) [V]. Vacek et al. 1994 (10 cadaver jaws, 171 surfaces) found sulcus 1.34 ± 0.84, JE 1.14 ± 0.49 and CT 0.77 ± 0.32 mm; the connective tissue attachment is the least variable component [V]. A common clinical guideline is about 3 mm from the restoration margin to the bone crest [U].
- **Gingival sulcus**: histologic depth is about 0.69 mm (Gargiulo). A healthy clinical **probing depth is ≤3 mm** (commonly 1-3 mm) [V].
- **CEJ relationships** [V]: three SEM patterns (cementum overlapping enamel, edge-to-edge, a gap exposing dentin) plus a rarer enamel-over-cementum pattern; the distribution is irregular even around one tooth (Neuvald & Consolaro 2000, 198 teeth). Commonly quoted proportions: overlap about 60-65%, edge-to-edge about 30%, gap about 5-10% [U for the exact percentages; sources vary].
- The CEJ (cervical line) curves toward the incisal or occlusal edge on the proximal surfaces. The curvature is greatest on the mesial of incisors (about 3 mm on the mandibular incisors) and about 1 mm less on the distal [V].

## 7. Dental implants
- **Components**: implant body or fixture (screwed into bone), abutment (transmucosal connector), abutment screw, and crown. Restorations are **screw-retained** (screw access channel through the crown) or **cement-retained** (crown cemented onto the abutment). A cover screw is used for submerged healing and a healing abutment for transmucosal healing.
- **Diameter** (definitions vary by paper):
  - ITI Consensus (Jung et al. 2018) narrow categories [V]: Cat 1 <2.5 mm ("mini"), Cat 2 2.5 to <3.3 mm, Cat 3 3.3-3.5 mm. Narrow-diameter implants of 2.5 mm or more had survival similar to standard-diameter implants. Those under 2.5 mm had lower survival.
  - A recent systematic review [V] used: extra-narrow <3.0, narrow 3.0 to <3.75, standard 3.75 to <5, wide ≥5 mm.
  - Practical summary: narrow <3.5 mm, regular about 3.75-4.5 mm, wide ≥5 mm.
- **Length**: common range is about 6-16 mm [U for range]. "Short" means **≤6 mm** in the ITI 2018 consensus [V]. Older literature used ≤8 or <10 mm. Short implants are valid when bone height is reduced but are less predictable [V].
- **Connections** [V]: **external hexagon** (original Brånemark design; more micromovement and microgap, more screw loosening). **Internal** (hex, octagon, tri-lobe; better force distribution to the screw). **Conical / Morse taper** (best mechanical and biological seal; usually combined with an internal index for anti-rotation, since a pure cone does not resist rotation). Platform switching means an abutment narrower than the implant shoulder.
- **Screw torque is manufacturer-specific. Do not generalize.** Example [V]: Straumann abutment-to-implant **35 Ncm**, restoration-to-abutment (occlusal screw) **15 Ncm**. Typical range across systems is about 15-35 Ncm (prosthetic and multi-unit screws are lower, abutment screws higher) [U for full range].
- **Minimum distances** [V unless noted]:
  - Implant to adjacent tooth: **≥1.5 mm**.
  - Implant to implant: **≥3 mm** (Tarnow, Cho & Wallace 2000, 36 patients: crestal bone loss 1.04 mm when implants were ≤3 mm apart vs 0.45 mm when >3 mm; lateral bone loss about 1.3-1.4 mm from each shoulder; external-hex implants without platform switching, but still recommended).
  - Buccal bone: **about 1.8-2 mm** often recommended (Spray et al. 2000, >3,000 implants: mean facial bone 1.7 mm; bone loss decreased and apposition became likely at ≥1.8 mm). A 2014 review found no validated minimum, and 1 mm is also cited.
  - Inferior alveolar canal: **2 mm safety margin** is the conventional rule. Tufekcioglu et al. 2017 (474 implants) found no significant difference in neurosensory complications at <2 vs >2 mm, but the three implants touching the canal (0 mm) caused paresthesia.
- **Osteotomy (implant bed)** [U, standard surgical teaching]: prepared with a pilot drill, then sequentially wider drills to the planned depth, under irrigation. The drill sequence, the final drill diameter (usually somewhat narrower than the implant, varying with bone density) and the drill lengths are **manufacturer-specific**. Do not present one sequence as universal.
- **Loading protocols** (ITI 2003 consensus, Cochran et al. 2004) [V]: immediate means within 48 h. Early means 48 h to 3 months. **Conventional means a 3-6 month healing period.** Classic Brånemark practice was about 3 months in the mandible and about 6 months in the maxilla [U]. Immediate loading protocols usually ask for an insertion torque of at least about 30 Ncm (Papaspyridakos et al. 2014 SR) [V].

## 8. Endodontics
- **Working length**: the apical constriction (minor foramen) is usually about **0.5-1 mm** short of the major foramen and radiographic apex. Kuttler 1955 found 0.5 mm (young) and about 0.8 mm (older) [V]. The major foramen can open up to 3 mm to the side of the anatomic apex [V]. Obturation within 0-2 mm of the radiographic apex is associated with the best outcomes. Electronic apex locators are used together with radiographs [V].
- **ISO files** [V]:
  - The size number = tip diameter **D0 in hundredths of a mm**. A size 25 file has D0 = 0.25 mm.
  - Standard **.02 taper**: diameter increases 0.02 mm per mm. **D16 = D0 + 0.32 mm**.
  - **16 mm cutting flutes** (D0 to D16).
  - Overall lengths **21, 25, 31 mm**. The extra length is non-cutting shaft.
  - Handles are colour-coded. The standard cycle is white, yellow, red, blue, green, black (15-40 etc.) [U].
  - NiTi rotary files often use greater tapers (.04, .06, variable).
- **Rubber dam** is the standard of care for root canal treatment. The ESE Quality Guidelines (2006) require it [V title; wording U].

## 9. Orthodontics basics
- **Bracket slot sizes**: **0.018 × 0.025 in** and **0.022 × 0.028 in** (the slot depths 0.025 and 0.028 in are [U]). Angle proposed the 0.022 in slot (1925). A systematic review (Vieira et al., Angle Orthod 2018; 4 trials) found no reliable evidence that either slot is more efficient, although most studies suggested shorter treatment with 0.018 in [V].
- **Archwires**: nickel-titanium (NiTi; superelastic, low force, used for initial alignment), stainless steel (stiff, used for working and finishing), TMA / beta-titanium (intermediate stiffness, formable), cobalt-chromium (Elgiloy) [U, standard textbook].
- **Force**: light, continuous forces are preferred, because heavy forces cause PDL hyalinization and undermining resorption [U, standard textbook].

## 10. Common AI mistakes to avoid
1. Saying adults have "28 teeth". The full permanent dentition is **32**. 28 applies only when third molars are excluded or missing.
2. Treating FDI and Universal numbers as interchangeable. **FDI 36 = lower left first molar = Universal 19.** Universal has no tooth 36. Universal 3 = FDI 16.
3. Starting Universal numbering at the wrong tooth. **#1 is the upper right third molar.** The numbering goes along the upper arch to #16, then drops to the lower left (#17) and runs back to #32 (lower right).
4. Mirroring left and right. Dental charts use the **patient's** right and left. On a facing (frontal) view the patient's right is on the viewer's left.
5. Saying enamel regenerates or "grows back". It is acellular and cannot regenerate. Only remineralization of early lesions is possible.
6. Giving primary teeth "no roots" or premolars. Primary teeth have roots that resorb. The primary dentition has **20** teeth and **no premolars**. Primary molars are replaced by permanent premolars.
7. Drawing all molars with 3 roots. **Maxillary molars have 3 roots (2 buccal + palatal). Mandibular molars have 2 (mesial + distal).** A third distolingual root (radix entomolaris) is a variation.
8. Giving maxillary molars "3 canals" as fixed. MB2 is present in most maxillary first molars (about 57-74%), so 4 canals is common. Second molars have MB2 less often (about a quarter to 40% on CBCT).
9. Giving the maxillary first premolar 1 root by default. **2 roots (buccal + palatal) is typical.** The mandibular first premolar usually has 1 root.
10. Confusing mesial and distal. **Mesial = toward the midline** along the arch. Distal = away from it. On a molar, mesial points toward the front of the mouth.
11. Using "buccal" for anterior teeth or "lingual" for maxillary teeth without care. Anterior teeth have a **labial** surface and posterior teeth a **buccal** surface ("facial" covers both). The inner surface of maxillary teeth is commonly called **palatal**.
12. Making the canine root short or average. The maxillary canine has the **longest root** (about 17 mm).
13. Drawing mandibular incisors wider than maxillary ones, or the lower central wider than the lower lateral. The lower central (about 5 mm) is the narrowest tooth.
14. Saying the first permanent tooth is a premolar or the canine. The **first molar (about 6 y)** and lower central incisors come first. The first molar erupts behind the primary 2nd molar without replacing any tooth.
15. Quoting one universal implant torque or one implant size. Torque and dimensions are **manufacturer-specific**.
16. Calling the 2.04 mm biologic width the "sulcus". It is the junctional epithelium + connective tissue (supracrestal attachment). The sulcus is additional (about 0.69 mm histologically).

## 11. Directional terminology [standard; U for wording]
- **Mesial**: toward the midline, following the arch. **Distal**: away from the midline.
- **Proximal** (interproximal): the mesial or distal surfaces facing adjacent teeth. The **contact area** is where neighbouring teeth touch.
- **Facial**: the surface toward the lips or cheek. **Labial** is used for anterior teeth, **buccal** for posterior teeth.
- **Lingual**: toward the tongue. **Palatal** is used for the maxillary arch.
- **Occlusal**: the chewing surface of premolars and molars. **Incisal**: the cutting edge of incisors and canines.
- **Apical**: toward the root tip. **Coronal**: toward the crown. **Cervical**: the neck region at the CEJ.
- **Long-axis thirds**: crowns are divided into cervical, middle and incisal/occlusal thirds. Roots are divided into cervical (coronal), middle and apical thirds.
- **Line angles and point angles**: for example, the mesiobuccal line angle and the mesio-buccal-occlusal point angle.
- **Standard anatomical views**: facial (labial/buccal), lingual, mesial, distal, and incisal/occlusal.
  - In anatomic position, maxillary crowns point down (roots up) and mandibular crowns point up (roots down). Both crowns point toward the occlusal plane. Some textbook single-tooth illustrations show every tooth crown-up, so state the orientation explicitly.
  - The occlusal view is drawn with the facial surface at the top or bottom, consistently. Textbooks orient the buccal surface toward the top of the page for occlusal views [U].
- **Intra-oral orientation**: the "arch view" of the maxilla is seen from below and the mandible from above. In charting diagrams, the patient's right is on the viewer's left.

## 12. Restoring root-filled teeth & canal-orifice anatomy
- **Ferrule**: a collar of sound dentin **1.5–2 mm high** (and about 1 mm thick [U]) wrapped by the crown improves fracture resistance of root-filled teeth; an incomplete ferrule beats none (Juloski et al. 2012 literature review, J Endod) [V]. An adequate ferrule lowers the influence of the post system, cement and crown on performance [V, same review].
- **Apical seal after post-space preparation**: leave **at least 4 mm** of gutta-percha apically (articles quote 4–6 mm; <3 mm associated with more periapical lesions) [V, Sci World J 2015 paper citing earlier studies].
- **A post does not reinforce the root**; it retains the core when too little coronal tooth remains [U, standard teaching].
- **Post dimensions**: commonly no wider than about ⅓ of the root diameter [U]. Length rules vary; do not state one rule as universal.
- **MB2 orifice**: on average **~1.9 mm (±0.6) mesial / mesio-palatal to MB1** along the line toward the palatal orifice (CBCT study, PMC6400017) [V]. It is often under a dentin shelf, outside a classic triangular access outline [U, standard teaching]. On a straight buccal radiograph MB1 and MB2 overlap; angled (SLOB) views or CBCT separate them [U, standard teaching].

## 13. Periodontium in health and disease
- **Health:** bone crest ~1.5–2 mm apical to the CEJ; sulcus probing depth ≤3 mm; supracrestal attachment ≈2 mm (section 6) [V].
- **Clinical attachment level (CAL)** is measured from the CEJ to the base of the pocket: **CAL = probing depth + recession** (subtract when the margin sits coronal to the CEJ) [U, standard definition].
- **2017 AAP/EFP staging** (worst interdental site) [V, Tonetti, Greenwell & Kornman 2018; table via dentalcare.com CE610]:
  | Stage | Interdental CAL | Radiographic bone loss | Complexity (examples) | Tooth loss from periodontitis |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | I | 1–2 mm | coronal third, <15% | max PD ≤4 mm, mostly horizontal | none |
  | II | 3–4 mm | coronal third, 15–33% | max PD ≤5 mm, mostly horizontal | none |
  | III | ≥5 mm | to the middle third and beyond | PD ≥6 mm, vertical loss ≥3 mm, **furcation Class II or III** | ≤4 teeth |
  | IV | ≥5 mm | to the middle third and beyond | Stage III complexity plus masticatory dysfunction etc. | ≥5 teeth |
  Grading (A/B/C, rate of progression) is a separate axis. Don't assign a stage from one tooth in a figure; say "consistent with Stage III" at most.
- **Furcation classes (Hamp, Nyman & Lindhe 1975)** [V for the source; cut-offs as commonly taught]: Class I = horizontal loss ≤3 mm; Class II = >3 mm but not through-and-through; Class III = through-and-through. (Glickman grades I–IV are an older alternative.)
- **Root trunk** (CEJ to furcation entrance) [U]: mandibular first molar about 3 mm buccally and about 4 mm lingually. The furcation opens to probing once attachment is lost past the trunk. In the kit, `spec.trunk` is the modelled trunk depth.
- **Bone loss patterns:** horizontal (crest lowered evenly) and vertical/angular (defect alongside a root). Lost bone does not regenerate spontaneously; regenerative procedures can restore some in suitable (e.g. narrow intrabony) defects [U, standard teaching].

## 14. Crown preparation (full coverage)
Reductions depend on the **crown material** and differ between sources and manufacturers. Show them as "typical" or "about", name the material, and never present one set as universal.

| Material | Incisal / occlusal | Axial (facial) | Finish line |
|---|---|---|---|
| Glass-ceramic (e.g. lithium disilicate), anterior | 1.5–2.0 mm (some guides 1.0–1.5) | 1.0–1.5 mm | Rounded shoulder or deep chamfer, ~1.0 mm [V, Ivoclar e.max chairside guide; U Toronto] |
| Metal-ceramic, anterior | ~2.0 mm | ~1.2–1.5 mm facial; lingual ≥0.7 mm (1.0 mm under porcelain) | Facial shoulder (radial), lingual chamfer [V, student guides and reviews; textbook values U] |
| Monolithic zirconia | 1.0–1.5 mm (minimum thickness quoted 0.5–1.0 mm) | ~0.5–1.0 mm | Light chamfer 0.3–0.5 mm (feather edge possible) [V, Spear; lab guides] |

- **Total occlusal convergence (taper)**: recommended about **10–20°** total (Goodacre, Campagni & Aquilino 2001, J Prosthet Dent review) [V]; 2–6° is the old, rarely achieved ideal. Measured student and clinical preps average ~15° anteriors and ~19° molars [V, CRDSP 2014 review; typodont study via search, U for exact numbers]. In the kit, `DK.prep` / `DK.prepMorph` keep the crown's own wall shape: say "slight taper", don't quote a degree off the model.
- **Wall height**: resistance needs enough axial wall; minimum occlusocervical height **4 mm for molars and 3 mm for other teeth**, with an occlusocervical-to-faciolingual ratio ≥0.4; add grooves or boxes when these are missing (Goodacre et al. 2001) [V].
- **Margin placement**: supragingival or at the gingival margin when esthetics allow; for esthetics, at most ~0.5 mm into the sulcus. Keep the margin clear of the supracrestal tissue attachment (≈2 mm above the bone crest, section 6) [U, standard teaching].
- **Anterior sequence (canine, incisor)** [U, standard teaching]: depth-orientation grooves → incisal reduction (canine: along both cusp slopes, edge angled ~45° lingually on maxillary teeth) → facial reduction **in two planes** (cervical and incisal halves follow the facial curvature) → lingual concavity with a wheel / football diamond (~0.7–1.0 mm) → lingual axial wall above the cingulum (keeps retention) → proximal reduction → margin refined, angles rounded.
- **Burs** [U, standard teaching]: tapered round-end diamond for axial walls and chamfers; flat-end tapered diamond for shoulders; wheel or football (egg) diamond for the lingual concavity of anteriors; fine-grit or flame diamonds for finishing. High speed with water spray.
- **Pulp**: young teeth have larger pulps; the remaining dentin over the pulp should stay thick enough to protect it (≥~1 mm is commonly quoted) [U]. Don't draw a prep that reaches a pulp horn.

### 15.2 How primary teeth differ from permanent teeth (modelling rules)
- **Enamel** is thin and of **even thickness, about 0.5–1 mm** (permanent 1.5–2.5 mm over cusps) [V]; a spectroscopic study gives means of 1.14 mm (primary) vs 2.58 mm (permanent) [V]. It ends abruptly at the cervix (a definite cervical ridge) rather than tapering [V].
- **Dentin** is thinner, so the **pulp is proportionally larger**, follows the DEJ closely, and the **pulp horns are longer and more pointed than the cusps suggest**; the **mesial (mesiobuccal) horn is the highest** [V]. Thin dentin over the horns is why primary molar preparations expose pulps easily.
- **Crowns** are short, bulbous and **constricted at the cervix**; primary molars are bell-shaped with pronounced buccal cervical ridges (strongest on the first molars) and a narrow occlusal table; the buccal and lingual surfaces converge occlusally [V].
- **Roots** are longer and more slender in relation to the crown; anterior roots are narrow mesiodistally; **molar roots flare out beyond the crown outline** to make room for the developing premolar crown between them, and there is **practically no root trunk: the furcation lies immediately below the CEJ** [V].
- **Cusp and root counts** [V]: primary maxillary first molar 4 cusps and 3 roots (MB, DB, palatal), unlike any permanent tooth; primary maxillary second molar resembles the permanent maxillary first molar (4 cusps plus a Carabelli tubercle, 3 roots); primary mandibular first molar is unique (4 cusps, 2 roots, strong mesiobuccal cervical bulge); primary mandibular second molar resembles the permanent mandibular first molar (5 cusps with **three buccal cusps of nearly equal size**, 2 roots).
- Colour whiter / bluish-white; **no mamelons** on the incisors [V].
- **Physiological root resorption** precedes exfoliation; the kit spreads it over the three years before the exfoliation age [U for the duration]. Resorption starts on the root surface facing the successor's crown (apical for incisors, inter-radicular for molars) [U].

### 15.3 Exfoliation, succession and the mixed dentition (eruption in §5)
Exfoliation (AAPD, Logan & Kronfeld) [V]: central incisors 7–8 y maxillary / 6–7 y mandibular; lateral incisors 8–9 / 7–8; canines 11–12 / 9–11; first molars 9–11 / 10–12; second molars 9–12 / 11–13. Permanent crown (enamel) completion [V]: incisors 4–5 y, canines 6–7 y, first premolars 5–6 y, second premolars 6–7 y, first molars 30–36 months, second molars 7–8 y; root completion: incisors 9–11 y, canines 12–15 y, premolars 12–14 y, first molars 9–10 y, second molars 14–16 y. Third molar calcification begins at 7–10 y [V].
- **Successors** [V]: primary incisors and canines are replaced by the permanent teeth of the same name; **primary first and second molars are replaced by the first and second premolars**. Permanent molars have no predecessors. FDI arithmetic: successor = primary number − 40 (54 → 14, 75 → 35).
- The successor crown forms **between the flared roots** of a primary molar and **lingual / apical** to a primary incisor or canine; its eruption drives the resorption [U, standard teaching].
- **Mixed dentition** runs from the first permanent tooth (about 6 y: mandibular central incisor and first molars) to the loss of the last primary tooth (about 12 y). At **age 8** a typical lower quadrant holds permanent 31, 32, 36 and primary 73, 74, 75, with 33, 34, 35 and 37 forming in the bone (`DK.dentitionAt(8, { jaw: 'mandible' })`) [V from the tables above].

### 15.4 Third molars
- **Dimensions**: Wheeler crown / root length maxillary 6.5 / 11 mm, mandibular 7 / 11 mm (§3) [V]; Wheeler crown widths MD 8.5, BL 10 (maxillary) and MD 10, BL 9.5 (mandibular) [U]. A caliper study of extracted teeth found maxillary crown 7.23, root 10.76, MD 9.53, BL 8.61 mm and mandibular 7.65, 10.71, 9.70, 8.63 mm [V]. Molar MD width decreases from first to third molar (§3).
- **Morphology** [V, Wheeler-based lecture]: third molars **vary more than any other teeth** and may bear little resemblance to their neighbours. The maxillary third molar is typically **heart-shaped** from the occlusal (3 functioning cusps: two buccal, one large lingual; distolingual cusp small or absent), with a shorter, narrower crown than the second molar and **roots fused into one tapered mass**, shorter and **inclined distally**. The mandibular third molar resembles the second molar but is smaller, with a shorter, often fused root portion, **bifurcation in the apical third**, more supplemental grooves, and irregular outlines. Say "variable" in any subtitle.
- **Agenesis** (at least one third molar missing): **22.63%** worldwide (95% CI 20.6–24.8%; 92 studies, 63,314 subjects; Carter & Worthington 2015) [V]; it varies by population (highest in Asian samples).
- **Impaction**: pooled prevalence **24.40%** of people (95% CI 19.0–30.8%; 49 studies, 83,484 subjects; Carter & Worthington 2016) [V]; odds **57.58% higher in the mandible** than in the maxilla; no sex difference [V]. Angulation of impacted third molars: **mesioangular 41.17%**, vertical 25.55%, distoangular 12.17%, horizontal 11.06% [V]. A 2024 meta-analysis of 98 studies found a higher pooled figure (36.9% per subject; Asia 43.1%, Europe 24.5%) [V].
- **Eruption** 17–21 y (AAPD 17–30 y; §5); eruption after 25 is considered unlikely; root formation can continue up to 3 years after eruption [V].

### 15.5 Impaction classifications
- **Winter (1926)**: the angle between the long axis of the third molar and that of the second molar: **vertical, mesioangular, horizontal, distoangular**, plus inverted, buccal / lingual (transverse) [V]. Angle bands used in radiographic studies [V, Dentistry 3000 2025]: **vertical −10° to +10°, mesioangular 11–79°, horizontal 80–100°, distoangular −11° to −79°**; "other" beyond ±100°. Mesioangular is the most common and distoangular the hardest to remove in the mandible [V]. Winter's classification is the more reproducible of the two systems [V].
- **Pell & Gregory (1933)**, mandible: *depth* **A** = occlusal surface level with (or above) the second molar's occlusal plane; **B** = between the occlusal plane and the second molar's cervical line; **C** = below the cervical line. *Ramus relation* **Class I** = enough space between the distal of the second molar and the anterior border of the ramus for the whole crown; **Class II** = less than the crown's MD width (the ramus covers part of the crown); **Class III** = the crown lies entirely within the ramus [V]. Some papers define the depth by the third molar's own CEJ relative to the bone level instead (A not buried, B partly, C fully) [V]: name the convention you draw.
- Shiller's method measures the angle to the occlusal plane instead (0–10° vertical, 11–70° mesial or distal) [V].

### 15.6 Arch form and arch width
- The human dental arch is well described by the **beta function** (Braun et al. 1998; mean r = 0.98 against 40 casts) with two parameters: the arch **width W at the distal of the second molars** and the **depth D** from that line to the incisal point [V]. The kit draws `z = D · (3.0314 · (0.5 + x/W)^0.8 · (0.5 − x/W)^0.8 − 1)` (the 0.8 exponents and the normalising constant 4^0.8 = 3.0314 are the published form **[U]**) and chooses D so that the arch perimeter equals the sum of the tooth widths.
- Arch widths, normal occlusion (cusp-tip landmarks; means vary by population by 1–3 mm) [V]:

### 15.7 Common AI mistakes (this topic)
1. "Primary teeth have no roots" or "their roots are short stubs". They have long, slender roots (root : crown about 1.8); the roots **resorb** before exfoliation, which is why exfoliated teeth look rootless.
2. Calling primary molars "premolars" or giving the primary dentition 24 teeth. 20 teeth, no premolars; the premolars replace the primary molars.
3. Numbering primary teeth with the permanent numbers, or 1–20. FDI uses quadrants 5–8 (51–85); Universal uses letters **A–T** (A = upper right second molar, clockwise to T = lower right second molar); Palmer uses A–E in the quadrant bracket. Lowercase letters or "A1" style hybrids are not standard.
4. Giving primary teeth permanent-tooth proportions: enamel 2 mm thick, a small pulp, a long root trunk. Enamel is about 1 mm, the pulp is large with high mesial horns, and the furcation sits just below the CEJ.
5. Saying the primary maxillary first molar "looks like a small permanent first molar". It has a unique 4-cusp form; it is the **second** primary molars that resemble the permanent first molars.
6. Treating the third molar as a copy of the second molar. It is smaller, more variable, often has fused and distally inclined roots, and is missing in about a quarter of people; impaction is the rule rather than the exception for mandibular third molars that do erupt late.
7. Mixing up Winter and Pell & Gregory: Winter = angulation; Pell & Gregory = depth (A/B/C) and ramus relation (I/II/III). "Class B mesioangular" mixes the two systems and is fine as a combined description, but never call a depth an angulation.
8. Mirroring a chart. The patient's right is on the viewer's left in every chart, occlusal view and frontal view; the upper arch is drawn above the lower.
9. Placing left and right teeth as the same mesh. Left teeth are mirror images of right teeth; `DK.arch` mirrors them automatically, a single-tooth left-side figure needs `scale.x = −1` (or a right-side tooth number).
10. Drawing the arch as a semicircle. The arch is parabolic / beta-shaped: the posterior segments run almost straight back, and the maxillary arch is wider and deeper than the mandibular arch.

## 16. Caries and direct restorations

### Detection and staging: ICDAS II / ICCMS [V]
Visual codes for a clean, dry surface (Gugnani et al. 2011; ICDAS/ICCMS, Ismail et al. 2015):

| ICDAS | What is seen | ICCMS stage | Radiograph (ICCMS) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Sound after 5 s of air drying | sound | — |
| 1 | First visual change in enamel: opacity only after prolonged drying, or confined to a pit/fissure | **initial** | RA1 outer ½ enamel, RA2 inner ½ enamel ± to the DEJ |
| 2 | Distinct change in enamel, seen wet ("white spot", may be brown) | initial | RA3 outer ⅓ of dentin |
| 3 | Localised enamel breakdown (micro-cavitation), no visible dentin | **moderate** | RB4 middle ⅓ of dentin |
| 4 | Underlying dark shadow from dentin, with or without enamel breakdown | moderate | RB4 |
| 5 | Distinct cavity with visible dentin (< half the surface) | **extensive** | RC5 inner ⅓ of dentin |
| 6 | Extensive distinct cavity with visible dentin (≥ half the surface); may reach the pulp | extensive | RC6 into the pulp |

- ICCMS merges the codes: initial = 1–2, moderate = 3–4, extensive = 5–6. Management: initial → non-operative (fluoride, sealants, hygiene); moderate → minimally invasive, no or minimal dentin removal; extensive → restoration [V, Ismail 2015].
- About 32 % of lesions radiographically in the outer third of dentin are cavitated, and about 72 % of those in the inner two thirds [V, Ismail 2015]. ICDAS does not map codes one-to-one onto histological depth; code 4 is usually deeper than 3 [V, Gugnani 2011].
- `DK.cariesLesion` stage scale: 0 sound, 1 white spot (ICDAS 1–2), 2 enamel breakdown (3), 3 dentin cavity (4–5), 4 deep (6), 5 pulp exposure. Fractions interpolate.

### Lesion shape and histology
- **Smooth-surface (proximal, cervical) enamel lesion**: cone with a broad base at the surface and the **apex toward the DEJ** (follows the enamel rods). [V, lecture texts after Sturdevant; the 2007 Malaysian review]
- **Pit-and-fissure enamel lesion**: starts on the fissure walls; in section a cone with the apex at the fissure and the **base toward the DEJ**, so it undermines more enamel and involves more dentin for the same opening. [V, same sources; U for exact proportions]
- **Dentin lesion**: once the DEJ is reached the lesion **spreads laterally along the DEJ** (higher organic content) and pulpally along the tubules: a second cone with its **base at the DEJ** and apex toward the pulp, undermining the enamel. [V] Some authors argue the DEJ spread begins before the enamel cone arrives [U, Bjørndal, via a 2025 modelling paper].
- **Enamel lesion zones** (from the advancing front outward): translucent zone (~1 % pore volume), dark zone (2–4 %), body of the lesion (5–25 %, the bulk of the mineral loss), and a **surface zone** (~1 %) that stays relatively intact until cavitation. [V for the four zones; U for the pore percentages, review-article values]. The kit draws the body as one chalky solid under an intact surface, not the four zones.
- **Dentin lesion zones**: outer **infected dentin** (soft, bacteria-laden, collagen irreversibly denatured, cannot remineralise) and inner **affected dentin** (demineralised, collagen intact, remineralisable). Clinically the boundary is judged by hardness, not colour. [V, Kidd / ICCC 2016] Bond strength to affected dentin is ~33 % lower and to infected dentin ~78 % lower than to sound dentin, so the periphery of a cavity must be on sound tissue [V, review via search].
- Slow lesions provoke **sclerotic (translucent) dentin** at the front and **reactionary (tertiary) dentin** on the pulp wall; fast lesions provoke little of either [V]. `DK.cariesLesion` adds a reactionary lens from stage ~2.5.
- Remineralisation is possible only while the surface is intact (ICDAS 1–2, "non-cavitated"); **a cavitated lesion cannot heal**, it can only be arrested, sealed or restored [V, ICCC 2016; ADA 2018].

### Minimal intervention: carious tissue removal (ICCC 2016 consensus) [V]
Schwendicke et al., Adv Dent Res 2016;28(2):58–67, and the terminology paper (Innes et al. 2016, 28(2):49–57):
- Carious tissue is removed only to make a durable, sealed restoration possible, never to "remove all bacteria". The cavity periphery (at the DEJ and the enamel margin) is prepared to **hard dentin** for a seal; over the pulp, soft or firm dentin is left.
- **Shallow or moderately deep lesions** (not into the inner third/quarter of dentin): **selective removal to firm dentin** (a hand excavator meets resistance; "leathery" tissue may remain pulpally). Weak recommendation.
- **Deep lesions** (inner third or quarter of dentin radiographically, risk of exposure): **selective removal to soft dentin** over the pulp in primary and permanent teeth; **stepwise removal** (re-entry after 6–12 months) is an option in permanent teeth. Strong recommendations.
- **Non-selective removal to hard dentin** ("complete excavation") is **no longer advocated**.
- Dentin hardness: soft (deforms, scoops easily), leathery (does not deform but lifts without much force), firm (resists an excavator; scratchy), hard (sound; a sharp instrument is needed). Hardness is the primary criterion; colour and moisture are secondary.
- Liners: calcium hydroxide or GIC liners are **not necessary** to control a sealed lesion; a liner may help limit monomer penetration and protect the remaining thin dentin under a resin composite (weak recommendation). A systematic review found calcium hydroxide liners give no clinical benefit after selective/stepwise removal [V]. In the kit, a liner is `DK.filling(tooth, prep, 'gic').slice(floor, floor + 0.5)`; use it as an option, not a default.

### Non-restorative management of non-cavitated lesions (ADA 2018) [V]
Slayton et al., JADA 2018;149(10):837–849: for non-cavitated coronal lesions the panel prioritises **sealants** (occlusal), **5 % NaF varnish**, 1.23 % APF gel, 5,000 ppm fluoride toothpaste, **resin infiltration** (approximal) and **38 % silver diamine fluoride** to arrest lesions; it advises against 10 % CPP-ACP products. ADA/AAPD 2016 sealant guideline: sealants prevent and arrest occlusal pit-and-fissure lesions in children and adolescents (≈73 % fewer new lesions than fluoride varnish alone); they **can be placed over non-cavitated (ICDAS 1–2) lesions**; no material (resin vs glass ionomer) is preferred [V].

### Cavity classification (G.V. Black) [V]
| Class | Site |
|---|---|
| I | Pits and fissures: occlusal of premolars and molars, buccal/lingual pits of molars, lingual pits of upper incisors |
| II | Proximal surfaces of premolars and molars (MO, DO, MOD) |
| III | Proximal surfaces of incisors and canines, incisal edge intact |
| IV | Proximal surfaces of incisors and canines involving the incisal angle |
| V | Gingival third of the facial or lingual surface of any tooth |
| VI | Cusp tips and incisal edges (a later addition, not in Black's original) |

### Cavity preparation dimensions (teaching values) [V where noted]
Columbia University operative manual (Class II amalgam, 2007) and lecture sources; Sturdevant values not reachable directly, so tagged [U] where only teaching:
- **Pulpal floor**: ~0.5 mm into dentin for amalgam (≈1.5–2.0 mm from the cavosurface at the fossa; amalgam under 1.5 mm thick fractures) [V]. For adhesive composite the floor only needs to clear caries: ~0.2–0.5 mm into dentin, "just past the DEJ" [U]. Pulpal floor flat, internal line angles rounded [V].
- **Isthmus**: as narrow as the caries allows; ~¼ of the intercuspal distance (Columbia: "just wider than the bur") [V, with sources ranging up to ⅓–½ for amalgam].
- **Proximal box**: dropped from the thinned marginal ridge along the DEJ; **gingival floor clears the adjacent tooth by ~0.5 mm**; **axial wall 0.5–0.8 mm into dentin** (Columbia) [V]; box slightly wider at the gingival than the occlusal (walls diverge gingivally); buccal and lingual walls meet the surface at 90° [V]. Axio-pulpal line angle rounded. Amalgam walls converge occlusally for retention; composite walls need no convergence (bonded) [U].
- `DK.cavityPrep` defaults: pulpal floor 0.3 mm below the DEJ at the fossa, isthmus max(1.4 mm, 0.22 × BL width), gingival floor at 0.28 × crown length, axial wall 0.6 mm inside the DEJ, box width 0.36 × BL width (≥ 2.4 mm), all overridable.
- **Matrix**: a Class II needs a matrix band to rebuild the missing wall before packing; sectional matrix + wedge (+ separation ring) is the usual choice for composite [U, standard teaching].
- **Composite**: placed and light-cured in **increments of ≤ 2 mm** (depth of cure; bulk-fill materials claim 4–5 mm but cure less reliably at 4 mm in hardness tests) [V, in-vitro studies via search]. Shrinkage is ~1–3 % by volume [U].
- **Amalgam**: condensed in increments and carved; needs ≥ 1.5 mm thickness and mechanical retention (convergent walls, undercuts) because it does not bond [V/U].
- **Sealant**: a low-viscosity resin (or glass ionomer) flowed into etched pits and fissures; a thin film that fills the fissure to its rims [V for use; thickness not specified in guidelines, the kit uses ~0.2 mm above the rim].
- **Burs**: flat-ended fissure (e.g. 245/330 pear) or tapered diamond at high speed for the outline and box; a large round bur at slow speed or a spoon excavator for caries removal [U, standard teaching].

### Amalgam safety (for the "mercury" question) [V]
FDA (24 Sept 2020): most people are not harmed by the small amount of mercury vapour; non-mercury alternatives are recommended **when possible** for pregnant and nursing women, women planning pregnancy, children under 6, people with neurological disease, impaired kidney function or mercury allergy. **The FDA does not recommend removing or replacing intact amalgam fillings** unless medically necessary; removal briefly raises exposure. ADA/AAPD reaffirmed amalgam as durable, safe and effective. The Minamata Convention (2013) commits countries to phase down (not ban) amalgam.

### Common AI mistakes (caries and fillings)
- Drawing a cavity as a black hole punched in the surface (in the kit a hole is empty space: `DK.holeCap(fig)` on its cut face, walls only in 3D). Early caries is a **white, chalky, intact surface**; the dentin lesion is much wider than the opening, spread along the DEJ, and the opening breaks in late. Brown/black is staining of a slow, arrested lesion, not the lesion itself.
- Enamel cone the wrong way: smooth-surface lesions point **toward** the DEJ; fissure lesions widen **toward** the DEJ.
- "Cavities heal with remineralisation." Only **non-cavitated** enamel lesions (ICDAS 1–2) can remineralise; once cavitated, a lesion can be arrested but the tissue does not grow back (enamel is acellular).
- "The dentist removes all the decay." Current consensus is **selective** removal: soft or firm carious dentin is deliberately left over the pulp in deeper lesions; only the periphery is cut to hard dentin.
- Drawing the pulpal floor deep into the pulp, or a box that touches the neighbour. Keep ~0.5 mm clearance and ≥ 1 mm of dentin over the pulp; cavity depth is set by the caries, not by a rule of 2 mm.
- Composite placed in one bulk and "set hard" by waiting. Light-cured composite is placed in ≤ 2 mm increments, each cured.
- "Amalgam fillings leak mercury and must be removed" and "silver fillings are banned." See FDA 2020 above; intact amalgam is left alone.
- Class numbering mix-ups: Class II is proximal on **posterior** teeth; Class III/IV proximal on **anterior** teeth; Class V is cervical, any tooth.
- Sealants only on sound teeth: sealants are also indicated over non-cavitated lesions (ADA/AAPD 2016).

## 18. Fixed prosthetics: bridges, veneers, inlays and onlays
Numbers depend on the material and the source; show them as "about" or "typical", name the material, and never present one set as universal. Tags: [V] confirmed on a fetched source listed under *Sources (§18)*; [U] standard teaching not pinned to a page.

### Bridges (fixed partial dentures, FDPs)
- **Terms** [U, Shillingburg / Rosenstiel]: **abutment** = the tooth (or implant) that supports the prosthesis; **retainer** = the part cemented or bonded to the abutment (usually a full crown; an onlay, inlay or a bonded wing in other designs); **pontic** = the artificial tooth replacing the missing one (Latin *pons*, bridge [V, Mir 2013]); **connector** = the joint between retainer and pontic (rigid, or non-rigid / precision attachment); **unit** = each retainer or pontic (a 3-unit bridge = 2 retainers + 1 pontic); **span** = the edentulous length bridged. A **cantilever** bridge has an abutment on one end only. A **resin-bonded (Maryland) bridge** holds the pontic by thin wings bonded to lingual/palatal enamel.
- **Indications** [U]: short spans (1–2 pontics), sound or already-restored abutments with enough crown height and periodontal support, a patient who is not a candidate for (or declines) an implant. Against: long spans, tilted or mobile abutments, large pulps in the young, poor hygiene, heavy parafunction, intact abutments (prefer an implant or a resin-bonded bridge).
- **Survival** [V, Pjetursson 2007]: conventional tooth-supported FDPs 93.8% at 5 years, 89.2% at 10 years. Cantilever FDPs about 81.8% at 10 years [U, Pjetursson 2004]. Resin-bonded bridges 91.3% at 5 years (debonding ≈15% at 5 years); zirconia-framework resin-bonded bridges 100% at 5 years in the pooled data; anterior single-retainer cantilever designs do best [V, Thoma 2017].
- **Resin-bonded (Maryland) bridge, modern design** [V, Kern 2023]: a single-retainer zirconia wing about **0.7 mm** thick, connector ≥ 3 mm high × 2 mm; the preparation is minimal and in enamel only (a shallow proximal box or rest, superficial enamel removal); retention is adhesive (air abrasion, MDP primer) with no mechanical "drilling into" the abutment. Classic metal wings 0.5–0.7 mm [U].
- **Ante's law** [V, Wikipedia; ADA/Dental Elf appraisal]: Ante 1926 ("the total pericemental area of the abutment teeth must equal or exceed that of the teeth to be replaced"), popularised in Johnston's *Modern Practice of Crown and Bridge Prosthodontics* (1938) [U]. **Contested**: teeth with a severely reduced but healthy periodontium can serve as abutments (Nyman & Lindhe) [U]; Lulic et al. 2007 (systematic review) found FDPs on severely reduced periodontal support survive about as well as others when periodontal health is maintained (10-year retention ≈95%) and concluded the law is **not supported** by evidence (evidence weak) [V appraisal; U for the exact figures]. Say "a traditional rule of thumb, not an evidence-based limit".

### Connectors (minimum cross-section by material)
| Material | 3-unit, anterior | 3-unit, posterior | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zirconia (3Y, IPS e.max ZirCAD IFU) | **9 mm²** | **12 mm²** | 2 pontics: 10 / 15 mm²; cantilever and Maryland: 10–15 mm²; vertical dimension greater than horizontal; basal (gingival) side rounded [V, Ivoclar ZirCAD IFU] |
| Lithium disilicate (IPS e.max Press / CAD) | **16 mm²** | **16 mm²** (to the second premolar only) | height ≥ width [U, Ivoclar parameter charts via search] |
| Metal-ceramic | ≈ 3 × 2.5–3 mm | ≈ 3 × 2.5–3 mm | textbook guidance; metal alone can be smaller [U] |
| Other zirconia guidance | 7 mm² | 9 mm² | lab guides; many lectures teach 3 × 3 mm anterior, 4 × 4 mm posterior [U] |

- **Height matters more than width**: beam stiffness scales with the cube of the occluso-gingival height [U, Shillingburg]; the ZirCAD IFU words it as "vertical dimension greater than horizontal" [V].
- **Gingival embrasure radius**: a rounded embrasure raises fracture load; in a monolithic zirconia test, 9 mm² connectors with a 0.9 mm radius broke at 1327 N vs 1054 N with a sharp 0.25 mm radius, and 12 mm² connectors were adequate either way; the authors advise caution with the 9 mm² minimum [V, Hafezeqoran 2020]. Never thin a connector when opening the embrasure for hygiene.
- In the kit: `DK.CONNECTORS` (zirconia 3 × 3 anterior / 4 × 3 posterior; lithium disilicate 4 × 4; metal-ceramic 3 × 2.5; gold 2.5 × 2.5 mm) and the label of `DK.bridge`'s connector are computed from these numbers.

### Pontic designs [V, Mir 2013 (Rosenstiel's classification); U where noted]
- **Mucosal contact**: **modified ridge lap** (contacts the ridge on the facial slope only, the lingual surface convex and clear of the ridge so floss passes: the default for anterior teeth, premolars and many molars); **ridge lap / saddle** (concave, hugs the ridge facially and lingually: uncleanable, **not recommended**); **ovate** (convex tissue surface seated in a prepared socket or ridge concavity, typically 1–1.5 mm into the tissue [U], giving an emergence-profile look: maxillary incisors, canines, premolars); **conical** (point contact, for thin ridges: molars without esthetic demand).
- **No mucosal contact**: **sanitary / hygienic** (≥ 3 mm clearance from the ridge; < 2 mm traps food; posterior mandible, minimum occluso-gingival thickness of the pontic ≈ 3 mm [U]); **modified sanitary (Perel)**: arched gingival surface that keeps the connectors tall while staying off the ridge [U].
- A pontic has **no root** and is **not attached to the gum**: it rests on or clears the mucosa and is carried by the connectors. In the kit, `DK.pontic` builds these shapes from the missing tooth's crown.

### Veneers (porcelain laminate veneers)
- **Types** [U]: **window** (intra-enamel, labial only, stops short of the incisal edge); **feathered / butt joint** (labial reduction carried to a reduced incisal edge, no palatal wrap); **incisal overlap / wrap** (edge reduced and the margin carried onto the palatal surface); "no-prep" and "minimal-prep" veneers exist for selected cases with space to add material [V, Smielak 2022 uses conventional vs no/minimal-prep categories].
- **Depths** (maxillary central incisor): labial about **0.3 mm cervical, 0.5 mm middle, 0.7 mm incisal third** so the whole prep stays in enamel; laterals 0.1–0.3–0.5 mm, canines 0.4–0.6–0.8 mm [U, Operative Dentistry 2022 via abstract]; Ferrari 1992 (114 extracted teeth): "preparation of enamel should be 0.5 mm… resin bonds better to enamel than to dentin" [V abstract]. Clinicians commonly under-prepare the middle and incisal thirds: use 0.3 / 0.5 mm depth-cutter burs or a silicone index [U, BDJ]. **Incisal reduction** for a butt joint or overlap about **1.0–1.5 mm** (some teach up to 2.0) [U].
- **Labial enamel thickness** of a maxillary central incisor: about 0.3–0.5 mm in the cervical third, 0.6–0.9 mm in the middle, 1.0–1.3 mm in the incisal third; CBCT: 0.48 ± 0.09 mm at 1 mm above the CEJ, 0.82 ± 0.16 mm at 3 mm (ages 18–40), thinner over 40 [U, PMC4283744; PMC11592583]. So a 0.3–0.7 mm prep stays in enamel only if it is shallower at the cervix.
- **Why enamel**: failures (debonding, fracture) are associated with large exposed dentin areas; all-enamel margins carry the lowest risk [V, Alenezi 2021]. **Margin**: a light chamfer ~0.3–0.5 mm at or just above the gingival margin [U]. **Veneer thickness** 0.3–0.7 mm (minimum ~0.3–0.4 mm for pressed / milled lithium disilicate) [U].
- **Survival**: cumulative 95.5% at 10 years (lower failure with incisal coverage) [V, Alenezi 2021]; pooled 89% at ~9 years, glass-ceramic 94% vs feldspathic 87% [U, Morimoto 2016]; 96% at 5–6 years, 93% at 10–11, 73% at 15–16 years [U, Layton & Walton 2007]. Veneers do not "last forever".

### Inlays and onlays
- **Definitions** [U]: an **inlay** sits within the cusps (intracoronal); an **onlay** covers at least one cusp; an **overlay / partial crown** covers all cusps. Cover a cusp when its remaining wall is thin (< ~2 mm) or the isthmus is wider than about half (two thirds: mandatory) of the intercuspal distance [U, Sturdevant rule for cast and amalgam].
- **Ceramic / CAD-CAM rules** [V, Ahlers et al. 2009 as summarised by Komet]: minimum occlusal depth **1.5 mm**; minimum isthmus width **2.5 mm** (other sources 1.5–2 mm [U]); wall divergence (opening angle) **6–10°** (other sources up to 15° [U]); internal angles rounded; **no bevels**, no feather edges, butt-joint (90°) cavosurface margins; minimum ceramic thickness **2 mm** (1.5 mm over non-functional cusps [U]); remaining proximal enamel wall ≥ 2 mm or include it; cusps reduced flat (horizontally) with rounded edges. Pulpal floor 1.5–2 mm below the central fossa; proximal box gingival floor ~1 mm wide, about 1 mm above the CEJ and clear of the contact (~0.5 mm) [U].
- **Cast gold** [U]: walls taper 2–5° per wall (Charbeneau), cavosurface and gingival bevels (gold is burnishable; ceramic is not), isthmus and depth as above.
- In the kit: `DK.inlayPrep` / `DK.inlay` take `{isthmus, depth, boxes, boxFloor, cusps, reduction}`; the walls diverge ~8° (both sides), internal angles are what the lattice gives (slightly rounded), and there are no bevels.

### Common AI mistakes (fixed prosthetics)
1. Calling a veneer a crown. A veneer covers the labial face (± the incisal edge) at 0.3–0.7 mm; a crown covers every surface with 1–2 mm reduction and a full margin.
2. Saying veneers need no tooth reduction. Only selected no-prep cases; a conventional veneer removes 0.3–0.7 mm of enamel, deeper toward the edge.
3. Saying a bridge is "attached to the gum" or "implanted". A conventional bridge is cemented to prepared abutment teeth; the pontic has no root and only rests on or clears the mucosa.
4. Saying a Maryland bridge is "drilled into" the teeth. Its preparation is minimal and in enamel; retention is adhesive.
5. Giving one connector size for every material. Zirconia 9 / 12 mm², lithium disilicate 16 mm², metal-ceramic ~3 mm high; height matters more than width.
6. Confusing inlay and onlay. Inlay = within the cusps; onlay = covers one or more cusps.
7. Saying porcelain veneers "last forever". About 95% at 10 years in good hands, with fracture, debonding and caries as the failures.
8. Quoting Ante's law as settled. It is a 1926 rule of thumb; the systematic review found no evidence for it.
9. Presenting the ridge-lap (saddle) pontic as standard. It is the design textbooks say not to use; modified ridge lap, ovate or sanitary are the choices.
10. Treating cantilever bridges as equal to conventional ones (≈82% vs ≈89% at 10 years), or lithium disilicate as suitable for molar bridges (it is limited to the second premolar).
11. Drawing a pontic with a root, a veneer on the lingual surface, or an onlay with bevelled ceramic margins.
