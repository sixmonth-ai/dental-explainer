# Dental facts reference (correction layer)

Correction layer for drawing 3D educational dental figures. Values are population averages; real teeth vary.
Tags: **[V]** = checked against a source listed at the end when this file was compiled (Oct 2026). **[U]** = unverified (a standard textbook value I could not confirm against a reachable source; treat as approximate).

---

## 1. Tooth numbering

### FDI / ISO 3950 (two-digit) [V]
- First digit = quadrant, second digit = position counted from the midline (1 = central incisor ... 8 = third molar).
- Permanent quadrants: **1** upper right, **2** upper left, **3** lower left, **4** lower right.
- Primary quadrants: **5** upper right, **6** upper left, **7** lower left, **8** lower right; positions 1-5 (central incisor ... 2nd molar). Primary teeth = 51-55, 61-65, 71-75, 81-85.
- Say the digits separately: tooth 36 is "three-six" (lower left first molar).
- Used by WHO and FDI and in most of the world (Europe, etc.).

### Universal / ADA [V]
- Permanent 1-32: **1 = upper right third molar**, counting along the upper arch to **16 = upper left third molar**, then **17 = lower left third molar** along the lower arch to **32 = lower right third molar**.
- Primary A-T: A = upper right 2nd molar ... J = upper left 2nd molar, K = lower left 2nd molar ... T = lower right 2nd molar.
- Used mainly in the USA.

### Palmer (Zsigmondy-Palmer) [V]
- A quadrant bracket symbol (┘ └ ┐ ┌) plus a number 1-8 counted from the midline (primary teeth A-E). Traditionally used in the UK, especially orthodontics.

### Mapping: permanent teeth (FDI ↔ Universal) [V]
"Right" and "left" are the **patient's** right and left.

## 2. Counts [V]
- Permanent: **32** teeth (16 per arch, 8 per quadrant), counting third molars. That is **28** if third molars are absent or extracted (agenesis is common).
- Per permanent quadrant: 2 incisors, 1 canine, 2 premolars, 3 molars (formula I2 C1 P2 M3).
- Primary: **20** teeth (10 per arch, 5 per quadrant): 2 incisors, 1 canine, 2 molars (I2 C1 M2). **There are no primary premolars.** Permanent premolars replace the primary molars.
- Permanent molars have no primary predecessors (they are "accessional" teeth).

Third molars (crown and root length) [V]: maxillary 6.5 / 11, mandibular 7 / 11. They are highly variable.

Proportion rules for modeling [V]:
- The maxillary canine has the longest root in the dentition (about 17 mm). The canines are the longest teeth overall (about 27 mm).
- The mandibular central incisor is the narrowest tooth (MD about 5 mm). It is narrower than the mandibular lateral incisor and is *wider labiolingually than mesiodistally*.
- The maxillary central incisor is wider MD (8.5) than LL (7.0). The maxillary lateral is about 2 mm narrower and shorter than the central.
- Maxillary molars are wider BL (11) than MD (10). Mandibular molars are wider MD (11) than BL (10.5). This is a key way to tell upper and lower molars apart in an occlusal view.
- Molar MD width decreases from 1st to 2nd to 3rd molar.

| Lateral incisor | 8-9 | 7-8 |

| 2nd molar | 12-13 (AAPD 12-14) | 11-13 |
| 3rd molar | 17-21 (AAPD 17-30) | 17-21 |

- The mandibular 1st molar and mandibular central incisor are usually the first permanent teeth to erupt, at about age 6.
- Mandibular teeth generally erupt before their maxillary counterparts. Premolars are a partial exception: the maxillary 1st premolar often erupts with or before the mandibular one.
- Root completion (AAPD): incisors about 9-11 y, canines 12-15 y, 1st molars 9-10 y, 2nd molars 14-16 y. The root completes about 2-3 years after eruption.

## 6. Tissue facts
- **Enamel**: thickest over cusps, about **2-2.5 mm** [V]. Some sources give up to about 3 mm. It thins to a knife or feather edge at the CEJ.
- **Composition by weight** [V]: enamel about **96% mineral** (hydroxyapatite), about 4% organic + water (non-collagenous proteins). Dentin about **70% mineral**, about 20% organic (mostly type I collagen), about 10% water. Cementum about **45-50% mineral**, about 50% organic + water. Source tables disagree slightly on the water and organic split.
- **Enamel is acellular and avascular.** Ameloblasts are lost at eruption, so enamel **cannot regenerate or repair itself biologically**. Only surface remineralization of the mineral is possible. Dentin is produced by odontoblasts that persist in the pulp, so secondary and tertiary dentin can form.
- **PDL width**: **0.15-0.38 mm** [V]. Commonly cited average is about 0.2 mm [U]. It is **thinnest in the middle third of the root** (the fulcrum) [V] and decreases with age.
- **Biologic width / supracrestal tissue attachment (STA)** [V]: Gargiulo, Wentz & Orban 1961 (287 teeth, 30 autopsy jaws). Mean sulcus depth was **0.69 mm**, junctional epithelium **0.97 mm**, connective tissue attachment **1.07 mm**, giving **JE + CT = 2.04 mm**. The 2017 AAP/EFP World Workshop renamed this "supracrestal tissue attachment". Individual variation is about 0.75-4 mm (Vacek 1994). A common clinical guideline is about 3 mm from the restoration margin to the bone crest.
- **Gingival sulcus**: histologic depth is about 0.69 mm (Gargiulo). A healthy clinical **probing depth is ≤3 mm** (commonly 1-3 mm) [V].
- **CEJ relationships** [V]: cementum overlaps enamel in about 60-65% of teeth, edge-to-edge in about 30%, and a gap exposing dentin in about 5-10%. Sources vary on these percentages.
- The CEJ (cervical line) curves toward the incisal or occlusal edge on the proximal surfaces. The curvature is greatest on the mesial of incisors (about 3 mm on the mandibular incisors) and about 1 mm less on the distal [V].

## 7. Dental implants
- **Components**: implant body or fixture (screwed into bone), abutment (transmucosal connector), abutment screw, and crown. Restorations are **screw-retained** (screw access channel through the crown) or **cement-retained** (crown cemented onto the abutment). A cover screw is used for submerged healing and a healing abutment for transmucosal healing.
- **Diameter** (definitions vary by paper):
  - ITI Consensus (Jung et al. 2018) narrow categories [V]: Cat 1 <2.5 mm ("mini"), Cat 2 2.5 to <3.3 mm, Cat 3 3.3-3.5 mm. Narrow-diameter implants of 2.5 mm or more had survival similar to standard-diameter implants. Those under 2.5 mm had lower survival.
  - A recent systematic review [V] used: extra-narrow <3.0, narrow 3.0 to <3.75, standard 3.75 to <5, wide ≥5 mm.
  - Practical summary: narrow <3.5 mm, regular about 3.75-4.5 mm, wide ≥5 mm.
- **Length**: common range is about 6-16 mm [U for range]. "Short" means **≤6 mm** in the ITI 2018 consensus [V]. Older literature used ≤8 or <10 mm. Short implants are valid when bone height is reduced but are less predictable [V].
- **Connections** [V]: **external hexagon** (original Brånemark design; more micromovement and microgap, more screw loosening). **Internal** (hex, octagon, tri-lobe; better force distribution to the screw). **Conical / Morse taper** (best mechanical and biological seal; usually combined with an internal index for anti-rotation, since a pure cone does not resist rotation). Platform switching means an abutment narrower than the implant shoulder.
- **Screw torque is manufacturer-specific. Do not generalize.** Example [V]: Straumann abutment-to-implant **35 Ncm**, restoration-to-abutment (occlusal screw) **15 Ncm**. Typical range across systems is about 15-35 Ncm (prosthetic and multi-unit screws are lower, abutment screws higher) [U for full range].
- **Minimum distances** [V unless noted]:
  - Implant to adjacent tooth: **≥1.5 mm**.
  - Implant to implant: **≥3 mm** (Tarnow, Cho & Wallace 2000: interimplant crestal bone loss when <3 mm; based on external-hex implants without platform switching, but still recommended).
  - Buccal bone: **about 1.8-2 mm** often recommended (Spray et al. 2000). A 2014 review found no validated minimum, and 1 mm is also cited.
  - Inferior alveolar canal: **2 mm safety margin** is the conventional rule. Tüfekçioğlu 2017 found no significant difference in neurosensory complications at <2 vs >2 mm, but paresthesia occurred when the implant touched the canal.
- **Osteotomy (implant bed)** [U, standard surgical teaching]: prepared with a pilot drill, then sequentially wider drills to the planned depth, under irrigation. The drill sequence, the final drill diameter (usually somewhat narrower than the implant, varying with bone density) and the drill lengths are **manufacturer-specific**. Do not present one sequence as universal.
- **Loading protocols** (ITI, 2003 definitions) [V]: immediate means within 48 h. Early means 48 h to 3 months. **Conventional means a 3-6 month healing period.** Classic Brånemark practice was about 3 months in the mandible and about 6 months in the maxilla [U].

## 8. Endodontics
- **Working length**: the apical constriction (minor foramen) is usually about **0.5-1 mm** short of the major foramen and radiographic apex. Kuttler 1955 found 0.5 mm (young) and about 0.8 mm (older) [V]. The major foramen can open up to 3 mm to the side of the anatomic apex [V]. Obturation within 0-2 mm of the radiographic apex is associated with the best outcomes. Electronic apex locators are used together with radiographs [V].
- **ISO files** [V]:
  - The size number = tip diameter **D0 in hundredths of a mm**. A size 25 file has D0 = 0.25 mm.
  - Standard **.02 taper**: diameter increases 0.02 mm per mm. **D16 = D0 + 0.32 mm**.
  - **16 mm cutting flutes** (D0 to D16).
  - Overall lengths **21, 25, 31 mm**. The extra length is non-cutting shaft.
  - Handles are colour-coded. The standard cycle is white, yellow, red, blue, green, black (15-40 etc.) [U].
  - NiTi rotary files often use greater tapers (.04, .06, variable).
- **Rubber dam** is the standard of care for root canal treatment. The ESE Quality Guidelines (2006) require it [V title; wording U].

## 9. Orthodontics basics
- **Bracket slot sizes**: **0.018 × 0.025 in** and **0.022 × 0.028 in** (the slot depths 0.025 and 0.028 in are [U]). Angle proposed the 0.022 in slot (1925). A 2017 systematic review (Vieira et al., Angle Orthod) found no conclusive efficiency difference between the two [V].
- **Archwires**: nickel-titanium (NiTi; superelastic, low force, used for initial alignment), stainless steel (stiff, used for working and finishing), TMA / beta-titanium (intermediate stiffness, formable), cobalt-chromium (Elgiloy) [U, standard textbook].
- **Force**: light, continuous forces are preferred, because heavy forces cause PDL hyalinization and undermining resorption [U, standard textbook].

## 10. Common AI mistakes to avoid
1. Saying adults have "28 teeth". The full permanent dentition is **32**. 28 applies only when third molars are excluded or missing.
2. Treating FDI and Universal numbers as interchangeable. **FDI 36 = lower left first molar = Universal 19.** Universal has no tooth 36. Universal 3 = FDI 16.
3. Starting Universal numbering at the wrong tooth. **#1 is the upper right third molar.** The numbering goes along the upper arch to #16, then drops to the lower left (#17) and runs back to #32 (lower right).
4. Mirroring left and right. Dental charts use the **patient's** right and left. On a facing (frontal) view the patient's right is on the viewer's left.
5. Saying enamel regenerates or "grows back". It is acellular and cannot regenerate. Only remineralization of early lesions is possible.
6. Giving primary teeth "no roots" or premolars. Primary teeth have roots that resorb. The primary dentition has **20** teeth and **no premolars**. Primary molars are replaced by permanent premolars.
7. Drawing all molars with 3 roots. **Maxillary molars have 3 roots (2 buccal + palatal). Mandibular molars have 2 (mesial + distal).** A third distolingual root (radix entomolaris) is a variation.
8. Giving maxillary molars "3 canals" as fixed. MB2 is present in most maxillary first molars (about 57-74%), so 4 canals is common.
9. Giving the maxillary first premolar 1 root by default. **2 roots (buccal + palatal) is typical.** The mandibular first premolar usually has 1 root.
10. Confusing mesial and distal. **Mesial = toward the midline** along the arch. Distal = away from it. On a molar, mesial points toward the front of the mouth.
11. Using "buccal" for anterior teeth or "lingual" for maxillary teeth without care. Anterior teeth have a **labial** surface and posterior teeth a **buccal** surface ("facial" covers both). The inner surface of maxillary teeth is commonly called **palatal**.
12. Making the canine root short or average. The maxillary canine has the **longest root** (about 17 mm).
13. Drawing mandibular incisors wider than maxillary ones, or the lower central wider than the lower lateral. The lower central (about 5 mm) is the narrowest tooth.
14. Saying the first permanent tooth is a premolar or the canine. The **first molar (about 6 y)** and lower central incisors come first. The first molar erupts behind the primary 2nd molar without replacing any tooth.
15. Quoting one universal implant torque or one implant size. Torque and dimensions are **manufacturer-specific**.
16. Calling the 2.04 mm biologic width the "sulcus". It is the junctional epithelium + connective tissue (supracrestal attachment). The sulcus is additional (about 0.69 mm histologically).

## 11. Directional terminology [standard; U for wording]
- **Mesial**: toward the midline, following the arch. **Distal**: away from the midline.
- **Proximal** (interproximal): the mesial or distal surfaces facing adjacent teeth. The **contact area** is where neighbouring teeth touch.
- **Facial**: the surface toward the lips or cheek. **Labial** is used for anterior teeth, **buccal** for posterior teeth.
- **Lingual**: toward the tongue. **Palatal** is used for the maxillary arch.
- **Occlusal**: the chewing surface of premolars and molars. **Incisal**: the cutting edge of incisors and canines.
- **Apical**: toward the root tip. **Coronal**: toward the crown. **Cervical**: the neck region at the CEJ.
- **Long-axis thirds**: crowns are divided into cervical, middle and incisal/occlusal thirds. Roots are divided into cervical (coronal), middle and apical thirds.
- **Line angles and point angles**: for example, the mesiobuccal line angle and the mesio-buccal-occlusal point angle.
- **Standard anatomical views**: facial (labial/buccal), lingual, mesial, distal, and incisal/occlusal.
  - In anatomic position, maxillary crowns point down (roots up) and mandibular crowns point up (roots down). Both crowns point toward the occlusal plane. Some textbook single-tooth illustrations show every tooth crown-up, so state the orientation explicitly.
  - The occlusal view is drawn with the facial surface at the top or bottom, consistently. Textbooks orient the buccal surface toward the top of the page for occlusal views [U].
- **Intra-oral orientation**: the "arch view" of the maxilla is seen from below and the mandible from above. In charting diagrams, the patient's right is on the viewer's left.

## 12. Restoring root-filled teeth & canal-orifice anatomy
- **Ferrule**: a collar of sound dentin **1.5–2 mm high and ~1 mm thick** wrapped by the crown improves fracture resistance of root-filled teeth (literature review, J Dent Med 2015) [V]. It matters more than the choice of post [U, consensus wording].
- **Apical seal after post-space preparation**: leave **at least 4 mm** of gutta-percha apically (articles quote 4–6 mm; <3 mm associated with more periapical lesions) [V, Hindawi 2015 review of cited studies].
- **A post does not reinforce the root**; it retains the core when too little coronal tooth remains [U, standard teaching].
- **Post dimensions**: commonly no wider than about ⅓ of the root diameter [U]. Length rules vary; do not state one rule as universal.
- **MB2 orifice**: on average **~1.9 mm (±0.6) mesial / mesio-palatal to MB1** along the line toward the palatal orifice (CBCT study, PMC6400017) [V]. It is often under a dentin shelf, outside a classic triangular access outline [U, standard teaching]. On a straight buccal radiograph MB1 and MB2 overlap; angled (SLOB) views or CBCT separate them [U, standard teaching].

## 13. Periodontium in health and disease
- **Health:** bone crest ~1.5–2 mm apical to the CEJ; sulcus probing depth ≤3 mm; supracrestal attachment ≈2 mm (section 6) [V].
- **Clinical attachment level (CAL)** is measured from the CEJ to the base of the pocket: **CAL = probing depth + recession** (subtract when the margin sits coronal to the CEJ) [U, standard definition].
- **2017 AAP/EFP staging** (worst interdental site) [V, dentalcare.com CE610]:
  | Stage | Interdental CAL | Radiographic bone loss | Complexity (examples) | Tooth loss from periodontitis |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | I | 1–2 mm | coronal third, <15% | max PD ≤4 mm, mostly horizontal | none |
  | II | 3–4 mm | coronal third, 15–33% | max PD ≤5 mm, mostly horizontal | none |
  | III | ≥5 mm | to the middle third and beyond | PD ≥6 mm, vertical loss ≥3 mm, **furcation Class II or III** | ≤4 teeth |
  | IV | ≥5 mm | to the middle third and beyond | Stage III complexity plus masticatory dysfunction etc. | ≥5 teeth |
  Grading (A/B/C, rate of progression) is a separate axis. Don't assign a stage from one tooth in a figure; say "consistent with Stage III" at most.
- **Furcation classes (Hamp)** [U, standard teaching]: Class I = horizontal loss ≤3 mm; Class II = >3 mm but not through-and-through; Class III = through-and-through. (Glickman grades I–IV are an older alternative.)
- **Root trunk** (CEJ to furcation entrance) [U]: mandibular first molar about 3 mm buccally and about 4 mm lingually. The furcation opens to probing once attachment is lost past the trunk. In the kit, `spec.trunk` is the modelled trunk depth.
- **Bone loss patterns:** horizontal (crest lowered evenly) and vertical/angular (defect alongside a root). Lost bone does not regenerate spontaneously; regenerative procedures can restore some in suitable (e.g. narrow intrabony) defects [U, standard teaching].

## 14. Crown preparation (full coverage)
Reductions depend on the **crown material** and differ between sources and manufacturers. Show them as "typical" or "about", name the material, and never present one set as universal.

| Material | Incisal / occlusal | Axial (facial) | Finish line |
|---|---|---|---|
| Glass-ceramic (e.g. lithium disilicate), anterior | 1.5–2.0 mm (some guides 1.0–1.5) | 1.0–1.5 mm | Rounded shoulder or deep chamfer, ~1.0 mm [V, Ivoclar e.max chairside guide; U Toronto] |
| Metal-ceramic, anterior | ~2.0 mm | ~1.2–1.5 mm facial; lingual ≥0.7 mm (1.0 mm under porcelain) | Facial shoulder (radial), lingual chamfer [V, student guides and reviews; textbook values U] |
| Monolithic zirconia | 1.0–1.5 mm (minimum thickness quoted 0.5–1.0 mm) | ~0.5–1.0 mm | Light chamfer 0.3–0.5 mm (feather edge possible) [V, Spear; lab guides] |

- **Total occlusal convergence (taper)**: recommended about **10–20°** total (Goodacre et al. 2001); 2–6° is the old, rarely achieved ideal. Measured student and clinical preps average ~15° anteriors and ~19° molars [V, CRDSP 2014 review; typodont study via search, U for exact numbers]. In the kit, `DK.prep` / `DK.prepMorph` keep the crown's own wall shape: say "slight taper", don't quote a degree off the model.
- **Wall height**: resistance needs enough axial wall; about 3 mm is the usual minimum for anteriors and premolars, about 4 mm for molars [U, standard teaching].
- **Margin placement**: supragingival or at the gingival margin when esthetics allow; for esthetics, at most ~0.5 mm into the sulcus. Keep the margin clear of the supracrestal tissue attachment (≈2 mm above the bone crest, section 6) [U, standard teaching].
- **Anterior sequence (canine, incisor)** [U, standard teaching]: depth-orientation grooves → incisal reduction (canine: along both cusp slopes, edge angled ~45° lingually on maxillary teeth) → facial reduction **in two planes** (cervical and incisal halves follow the facial curvature) → lingual concavity with a wheel / football diamond (~0.7–1.0 mm) → lingual axial wall above the cingulum (keeps retention) → proximal reduction → margin refined, angles rounded.
- **Burs** [U, standard teaching]: tapered round-end diamond for axial walls and chamfers; flat-end tapered diamond for shoulders; wheel or football (egg) diamond for the lingual concavity of anteriors; fine-grit or flame diamonds for finishing. High speed with water spray.
- **Pulp**: young teeth have larger pulps; the remaining dentin over the pulp should stay thick enough to protect it (≥~1 mm is commonly quoted) [U]. Don't draw a prep that reaches a pulp horn.

## 17. Periodontal disease
Builds on §6 (biologic width / supracrestal attachment, sulcus) and §13 (healthy crest, CAL definition, staging table, Hamp classes, root trunk). Don't repeat those; this section adds the disease geometry, the probe, the classifications and the sources.

### Health (what the healthy side of a figure shows)
- **Gingival health** (2017 World Workshop, Chapple / Lang & Bartold): probing depth ≤ 3 mm and bleeding on probing at < 10 % of sites on an intact periodontium; gingivitis is BOP ≥ 10 % of sites (localized 10–30 %, generalized > 30 %) [V, EFP / Colgate summary of Chapple et al. 2018].
- **Crest position**: 1–2 mm (often quoted 1.5–2 mm) apical to the CEJ on a radiograph; Hausmann et al. 1991 call < 1.5 mm CEJ-to-crest "no bone loss" regardless of percentage [V, secondary sources citing Hausmann 1991]. Draw the healthy crest at −1.8 to −2 mm.
- **Gingival margin**: on enamel, about 1–2 mm coronal to the CEJ in young adults, so the sulcus base lies near the CEJ and **CAL = 0** [U, standard teaching]. In a figure: margin +2, base 0 → PD 2 mm, CAL 0.
- **Where the probe stops**: histologic sulcus ≈ 0.69 mm (§6), but the probe penetrates the junctional epithelium. In health it stops coronal to the apical end of the JE; with inflammation it passes through the inflamed tissue to the most coronal intact dento-gingival fibres, about **0.3–0.5 mm apical to the JE** (Listgarten 1980). So probing depth seldom equals histologic pocket depth, and the discrepancy grows with inflammation [V].

### Periodontitis: definitions and the 2017 AAP/EFP classification
- **Case definition**: interdental CAL detectable at ≥ 2 non-adjacent teeth, or buccal/oral CAL ≥ 3 mm with pocketing > 3 mm at ≥ 2 teeth, not explained by other causes (cervical restorations, root fracture, trauma, third-molar extraction) [V, dentalcare CE610 / workshop summaries; wording of the Papapanou 2018 consensus U].
- **Staging** (severity + complexity, from the worst site; full table in §13) [V]: Stage I CAL 1–2 mm, RBL coronal third < 15 %, PD ≤ 4; Stage II CAL 3–4 mm, RBL 15–33 %, PD ≤ 5; Stage III CAL ≥ 5 mm, RBL to the middle third and beyond, PD ≥ 6, vertical bone loss ≥ 3 mm, furcation Class II–III, ≤ 4 teeth lost; Stage IV = Stage III plus ≥ 5 teeth lost, masticatory dysfunction, secondary occlusal trauma (mobility ≥ 2), severe ridge defect, drifting, < 20 remaining teeth. **Extent**: localized < 30 % of teeth, generalized ≥ 30 %, molar/incisor pattern [V, dentalcare CE610 after Tonetti, Greenwell & Kornman 2018].
- **Grading** (rate of progression) [V]: direct evidence over 5 years: Grade A no loss, B < 2 mm, C ≥ 2 mm; indirect: **% bone loss ÷ age** < 0.25 (A), 0.25–1.0 (B), > 1.0 (C); modifiers smoking (< 10 / ≥ 10 cigarettes a day) and diabetes (HbA1c < 7 / ≥ 7 %). Grade B is the default, moved by evidence.
- **Radiographic bone loss (%)** [U, common clinical convention]: (CEJ-to-crest − ~2 mm normal) ÷ root length (CEJ to apex) × 100. Some papers divide crest-to-apex by root length instead; the kit's `DK.perioMeasure` uses the first. Radiographs under-read true loss (2-D projection) [U].
- **Gingivitis vs periodontitis**: gingivitis is inflammation **without** attachment or bone loss and is reversible with plaque control; a swollen margin can give a probing depth > 3 mm with CAL 0 (**pseudo-pocket**). Periodontitis means attachment loss; the lost attachment and bone do **not** regenerate spontaneously after scaling, which arrests the disease rather than rebuilding support. Regeneration (GTR, enamel matrix derivative, grafts) can rebuild part of a **contained intrabony defect**; horizontal (suprabony) loss is not amenable to it [V for regeneration vs horizontal loss (reviews); reversibility of gingivitis U, standard teaching].

### Patterns of bone loss (what to draw)
- **Horizontal**: the crest lowered evenly, parallel to the CEJ line (suprabony pockets) [U].
- **Vertical / angular (infrabony)**: a defect alongside one root, classified by the remaining bony walls (Goldman & Cohen 1958): **3-wall** (bounded by bone on three sides, the root on the fourth; best for regeneration), **2-wall** (the interproximal **crater** between two teeth with the buccal and lingual walls kept, the most common pattern), **1-wall** (**hemiseptum**: only the proximal wall on the neighbour's side remains), and combined defects [V, secondary reviews; original paper not reached]. In a mesio-distal section an angular defect reads as a V beside the root; vertical loss ≥ 3 mm is a Stage III complexity factor.
- **Furcation involvement** (multi-rooted teeth): **Hamp, Nyman & Lindhe 1975**: Degree I horizontal probing < 3 mm; Degree II ≥ 3 mm but not through-and-through (a cul-de-sac); Degree III through-and-through [V, Wikipedia after Vandersall 2007; some secondary sources phrase it as thirds of the tooth width]. **Glickman 1953**: Grade I incipient (pocket into the flute, bone intact); Grade II cul-de-sac with horizontal loss but bone at the dome; Grade III through-and-through, covered by gingiva; Grade IV through-and-through and visible because of recession [V, Wikipedia after Knowles 1979]. Furcation Class II–III raises a case to Stage III.
- **Root trunk** of the mandibular first molar: measured means ~1.4–2.6 mm buccally and ~2.0 mm lingually (J Appl Oral Sci 2012, n = 233: 1.37 ± 0.78 B, 2.04 ± 0.89 L; another study 2.59 B) versus the textbook ~3 B / 4 L in §13; the lingual trunk is longer and its entrance narrower [V]. **Furcation entrance width**: ≤ 1.0 mm in 81 % and ≤ 0.75 mm in 58 % of molars (Bower 1979), narrower than most curettes [V]. Once the crest sits past the trunk the furcation is open to probing: draw it with the kit's `spec.trunk` (3 mm for 36).

### Gingival recession
- Recession = the margin apical to the CEJ, exposing root (cementum, then dentin): measure CEJ → margin. Then **CAL = PD + recession** [V, Cairo 2011 definitions].
- **Cairo 2011 (RT1–3)**, the classification the 2017 workshop adopted [V]: **RT1** no interproximal attachment loss (interproximal CEJ not detectable); **RT2** interproximal loss ≤ buccal loss; **RT3** interproximal loss > buccal loss. Inter-rater ICC 0.86 in the original 116-site study. Complete root coverage is predictable in RT1, partial in RT2, not expected in RT3 [V for the RT definitions; coverage expectations U, standard teaching]. (Miller I–IV is the older scheme: RT1 ≈ Miller I–II, RT2 ≈ III, RT3 ≈ IV.)

### Calculus
- **Supragingival**: white to yellowish-white, clay-like, forms above the margin opposite the salivary ducts: lingual of the lower incisors and buccal of the upper molars [V]. **Subgingival**: dark brown to greenish-black (blood pigments), hard and dense, on the root inside the pocket, invisible without probing or a radiograph [V]. Calculus is 70–90 % inorganic (mostly calcium phosphates: hydroxyapatite, whitlockite, octacalcium phosphate, brushite) [V]. Calculus is mineralised biofilm: a plaque-retentive factor, not the cause by itself [U].

### The periodontal probe
- **UNC-15** (University of North Carolina): 15 mm working end with a mark every millimetre and black bands at 4–5, 9–10 and 14–15 mm; tip Ø about 0.5 mm (0.48 mm in one manufacturer's spec) [V, retailer / manufacturer listings; Braz J Oral Sci 2024 compared tips between makers]. Other patterns: Williams 1-2-3-5-7-8-9-10 mm; Michigan O 3-6-8; Marquis bands 3–6 and 9–12; Nabers (curved) for furcations [V].
- **WHO / CPITN / BPE probe**: 0.5 mm ball end, black band 3.5–5.5 mm, further marks at 8.5 and 11.5 mm; probing force 20–25 g. BPE codes: 0 healthy, 1 bleeding, 2 calculus or overhang, 3 band partly in the pocket (PD 4–5.5 mm), 4 band disappears (PD ≥ 6 mm; some sources say ≥ 5.5), * = furcation [V, Ergodenta BPE guide + slide sets; the code-4 threshold differs between sources].
- **Force**: about **0.20–0.25 N (20–25 g)**, the usual study standard; heavier forces push the tip into the connective tissue and over-read, especially in inflamed tissue. Students average 30–35 g with a UNC-15 [V, Saudi Dent J 2014; Acta Med Philipp 2022]. Pressure-sensitive probes exist [V].
- **Technique** [V for sites and walking; U for the angle]: six sites per tooth (disto-, mid- and mesio-facial; the same lingually); keep the probe parallel to the long axis of the root; "walk" it in ~1 mm steps around the tooth; under a contact point angle it slightly so the tip reaches the col. Record the deepest reading per site; PD is read at the gingival margin.

### Common AI mistakes (periodontal)
1. Measuring probing depth from the CEJ. **PD is from the gingival margin; CAL is from the CEJ.** They coincide only when the margin sits at the CEJ.
2. Calling gingivitis "early periodontitis". Gingivitis has **no attachment loss** and is reversible; a swollen margin gives a pseudo-pocket with CAL 0.
3. Letting bone "grow back" after cleaning. Scaling stops the loss; it does not rebuild bone. Only regenerative surgery in a contained (intrabony) defect restores some.
4. Staging from one tooth. Stage and grade are whole-mouth diagnoses; one tooth is "consistent with Stage III".
5. Confusing the axes: CAL and bone loss set severity; PD ≥ 6, vertical defects ≥ 3 mm and furcation II–III are **complexity**; tooth loss separates III from IV; grade is the rate (% bone loss ÷ age), not severity.
6. Drawing the healthy crest at the CEJ. It sits 1–2 mm apical.
7. Recession without root exposure, or a pocket base coronal to the CEJ with "recession". Recession means the margin is apical to the CEJ and root is exposed; CAL then = PD + recession.
8. Furcation involvement on single-rooted teeth, or on a molar whose bone is still above the furcation. The crest must be apical to the root trunk (2–4 mm on a lower first molar).
9. Pale supragingival calculus inside a pocket, or black calculus on the crown. Subgingival calculus is dark and on the root; supragingival is pale and at the margin.
10. A probe drawn without markings, or with Williams marks called UNC-15.
